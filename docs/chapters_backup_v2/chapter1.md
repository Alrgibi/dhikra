# Chapter 1 — General Introduction

This thesis reports the design, construction and evaluation of ذِكرى (Dhikra), a speech-based cognitive screening instrument built for a setting that has no memory clinics, no reliable network and no specialist staffing to assume — and it reports that evaluation under the conditions such a deployment would actually impose. Dhikra extracts 117 speech and language measures across five families. Its externally evaluated English screening model uses a frozen 64-feature transcript-derived subset of linguistic, information-content and discourse-semantic measures; 53 acoustic measures support a separate language-independent pathway. The chapters that follow argue that the second half of that sentence is where the contribution lives: what separates a screening number that can be acted on from one that cannot is not the model behind it but the evidence regime around it.

## 1.1 Introduction

Dementia is a syndrome of progressive cognitive decline beyond ordinary ageing, and the World Health Organization names Alzheimer's disease as its most common form, contributing an estimated 60 to 70% of cases [1]. Among its earliest observable signs are changes in language: word-finding difficulty, thinning informational content, simplified syntax and looser reference appear in ordinary speech years before formal diagnosis, and they are measurable in language production itself rather than only in structured testing — the foundational evidence, from writing produced decades before disease, is reviewed in section 2.1. That is the premise of speech-based screening: a few minutes of ordinary speech, elicited by simple tasks and measured objectively, can carry a signal worth acting on. What screening means here is fixed at the outset, because every later claim inherits it. A screening instrument answers one triage question — does this person's speech resemble that of people with cognitive impairment closely enough that a clinician should look — and its output is an invitation to assessment. Diagnosis is a clinician's act, and nothing in this thesis performs it, approaches it, or substitutes for it.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

The problem this work addresses is an evidence gap, and the gap is documented rather than asserted. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of dementia prevalence across the Middle East and North Africa pooled 52 studies covering 1,045,908 people and reported a regional prevalence of 12.16% (95% CI 9.61 to 14.96), with wide country-level variation — Israel 17.00%, Iran 13.20%, Turkey 11.40%, Saudi Arabia 8.34%, Egypt 6.86% [2]. Libya is not among the countries it reports. A companion narrative review by overlapping authors states the reason in so many words: "Libya and West Bank and Gaza are not included due to the lack of recently published data" [3]. The gap is not new — an earlier systematic review of dementia epidemiology across the Arab world searched for Libyan studies and found none eligible, with prevalence studies existing only for Egypt, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Tunisia and the United Arab Emirates [4]. No population-level dementia prevalence study has been conducted in Libya.

	That absence is the problem statement, and it is stated at exactly that strength: nobody knows how common dementia is in Libya, because it has never been measured. The instruments that would measure it — validated cognitive screens, specialist assessment pathways, research corpora of Libyan speech — largely do not exist either, and the two absences feed each other: prevalence cannot be estimated without screening at scale, and screening at scale is not built for a population no instrument has been validated in. This thesis attacks the instrument half of that circle, with the tools of biomedical engineering, and is explicit throughout about which parts of the attack are validated evidence and which are prepared groundwork.

## 1.3 Motivations

The case for a speech-based screen, rather than another translated questionnaire, rests on reach. A systematic review of natural-language processing for dementia records that 60% of dementia patients live in low- and middle-income countries, and separately that the datasets and systems of this field are overwhelmingly English and drawn from high-income settings [5]. The language argument of this thesis follows from that second finding: the populations where the burden concentrates are the populations the field's instruments were not built for, and Arabic — for all its speakers — has essentially no presence in the field's datasets (section 2.5). Speech is the one clinical signal that needs no laboratory, no imaging and no specialist to collect: a recording made on an ordinary phone in a home carries it. An instrument that reads that signal, runs on hardware a Libyan clinic already owns, and explains its output in terms a family can understand is the motivating design, and the deployment constraints it must satisfy are stated next, because they are unusually strict and are not stated as modesty.

### 1.3.1 Why The Design Constraints Are Severe

The design constraints that follow are unusually severe for a diagnostic-adjacent system: no dedicated hardware, no network dependency after installation, and a paper form that reproduces the task battery when no device is available. They are not stated as modesty. Cognitive impairment is recognised in disability-inclusive disaster risk reduction as a condition that compounds vulnerability, and the evidence runs in both directions: a systematic scoping review of twenty-eight studies reports measurable cognitive decline in older adults with dementia following natural disasters, including working-memory loss persisting six months after hurricane exposure and elevated mortality associated with evacuation [6]. The populations in which cognitive screening is least available and the populations in which disruption does the most cognitive harm are substantially the same populations. A screening instrument that requires a functioning clinic, a reliable network and a trained specialist is therefore not a screening instrument for the setting that most needs one, and this work treats the absence of that infrastructure as a design input rather than as a deployment problem to be solved later.

## 1.4 Aim And Objectives

The aim of this work is to build a deployable speech-based cognitive screening instrument for a setting with no memory-clinic infrastructure, and to evaluate it under the conditions deployment would impose: data the model has never trained on, an operating threshold fixed in advance, and a single scoring that cannot be repeated or revised. The objectives through which that aim was pursued are the following.

1. Build a speech-feature extraction pipeline for cognitive screening.
2. Train and validate across multiple independent clinical corpora.
3. Establish generalisation via a locked external test set.
4. Adapt the method to Arabic.
5. Produce a deployable, quality-gated assessment platform.

	These objectives are reproduced as registered at the project's proposal, unchanged. Where the work exceeded an objective — the evaluation governance of Chapter 3 grew from a means into a contribution ranked first among the conclusions of section 6.1 — and where it narrowed one — the Arabic adaptation of objective 4 is delivered at the design level, with its validation specified rather than performed — the relevant chapter says so plainly, so that the distance between what was registered and what was achieved is disclosed rather than tidied away.

## 1.5 Scope And Limitations

Four boundaries fix the scope, and each is stated here because every later chapter inherits it. First, this is screening, not diagnosis: the instrument's positive output means a clinician should look, and never more. Second, it reads present state, not future risk. The distinction was fixed by measurement rather than preference: prediction of impairment nine years ahead from healthy speech was tested on 938 speakers and returned AUC 0.548 — chance — and that negative result closed the prospective ambition and scoped the instrument to detecting impairment that exists now (section 5.8). Third, the validated instrument is English-language: it was trained and externally evaluated on English picture-description corpora, and every performance figure in this thesis is an English-language figure. The Arabic component is a method awaiting validation — a culturally and linguistically adapted battery, stimuli drawn for the deployment culture, and a replacement marker engineered for Arabic's grammar — prepared for Libyan validation, not validated in Libya. No Libyan data exists in this work; a validation protocol is prepared and ethics-ready (Appendix B), and the corpus its full validation requires is specified in Appendix I. Fourth, the system has never been used with a real patient, and its safety architecture is built on that fact: where its evidence ends, it refuses rather than extrapolates (section 4.5).

## 1.6 Structure Of The Project

Chapter 2, Literature Review, reviews the evidence that language change precedes diagnosis, the systems built on that evidence, and the corpora they are evaluated on, and makes the comparison that positions this work: the field's high published figures live in an evaluation regime never shown to transfer between corpora, and this thesis reports in the regime that has. Chapter 3, Methodology, describes the corpora, features and evaluation protocol, and then the part that carries the thesis: the external test-set governance and the failure that produced it, pre-registration as a working method including where it failed, and the derivations that fix what the results can mean. Chapter 4, System Design, describes the instrument built around the frozen model — the platform, the task battery and its stimuli, the report, and the boundaries at which the system refuses to produce anything — under the constraint that it must degrade gracefully from a screening score — a development-calibrated probability estimate — to an indicator profile to a paper checklist. Chapter 5, Results and Discussion, reports the validated instrument: the development and external evaluations with their confounds and calibration, the diagnosis of the instrument's weakest number, fifteen negative results in one register, the measurement properties, and the probes that belong to the successor, reported under the rule of section 1.7. Chapter 6, Conclusion and Future Work, states what was learned and what it is worth, the limitations, the staged validation design and deployment path, the Arabic contribution in its three parts, and the closing claim; nothing in it is externally validated, and its first sentence says so. The ten appendices carry the feature definitions, the pilot protocol, the model card, the reporting-guideline self-assessment, a sample report, the levels-of-evidence register, the transcription-artefact audit, the risk-adjustment evidence base, the corpus specification, and the software and reproducibility record.

## 1.7 How Results Are Reported In This Thesis

This thesis contains two kinds of number, and they are not comparable. The distinction is not deployed versus experimental — that describes how code is used, which is not the property that matters. The property that matters is external validation, and in this project it is scarce in a specific and permanent way. Validated: the frozen English screening model was evaluated exactly once, on an external corpus, at a threshold fixed in advance. Locked external performance: AUC 0.8533 [0.7371, 0.9458], sensitivity 96.2%, specificity 33.3%. Subsequent threshold-transport analysis recovered external specificity to 77.8% by control-referenced thresholding — a post-hoc methodological contribution, not preregistered, not deployed, and requiring prospective validation. The corpus was excluded from the training data of the final model and from every modelling decision after the lock. Before the lock, five exploratory scorings occurred, one of which informed the decision to include Delaware in the development pool (section 3.9). That evaluation cannot be repeated: the corpus is spent, and repeating it would destroy the property that makes it worth having. Specified: every other configuration measured in this project — the task-genre findings, the transfer ablations, the acoustic path, the threshold rule, the Arabic instrument. Each has development-set evidence, and none has external validation, nor can any of them ever obtain it, because the corpus that would provide it has been spent.

	Chapter 5 reports the validated instrument. Chapter 6 reports the specified successor, and its first sentence says that nothing in it is externally validated. The structure is deliberate: a specified configuration that outperforms the validated one is the proper output of a future-work chapter, not an embarrassment. A thesis organised as the deployed system plus its experiments would invite the question of why the weaker configuration was deployed, and would force a governance argument into the middle of a results chapter; organised as validated and specified, the same finding is the deliverable it should be. One rule enforces the distinction mechanically, and it is never broken.

> **No unvalidated figure appears in the same table as a validated one. Not with a footnote, not with an asterisk, not in an adjacent column.**

	Different chapters, different tables, every time. The moment the two share a table, the reader must hold the distinction themselves — and by the account of this field's own review literature, readers do not, which is part of how a decade of reported performance above ninety per cent coexists with no tool in clinical use (section 2.3).
