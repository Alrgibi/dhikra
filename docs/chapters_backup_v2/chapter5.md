# Chapter 5 — Results and Discussion

This chapter is organised around the diagnosis of the instrument's weakest number. Locked external performance: AUC 0.8533 [0.7371, 0.9458], sensitivity 96.2%, specificity 33.3%. Subsequent threshold-transport analysis recovered external specificity to 77.8% by control-referenced thresholding — a post-hoc methodological contribution, not preregistered, not deployed, and requiring prospective validation. Sections 5.15 to 5.21 establish that the specificity was not a failure of the model but a failure of what the threshold was referenced to. Three findings carry that claim. Scored by recovery fraction — the specificity a threshold rule achieved, divided by the most any threshold could achieve at the sensitivity it actually reached — the median across the eighty rule-by-target-by-direction cells is 0.981: a rule that lands near its intended sensitivity captures essentially all the specificity the curve permits, so the rules were not inefficient, they were mis-placed (section 5.16). A threshold referenced to a local healthy-control distribution delivers specificity to target in every cohort tested — 79.8% on Pittsburgh, 79.9% on Delaware and 77.8% on the external corpus at an 80% target (section 5.18). And the false positives that produced 33.3% were real: the eighteen misclassified external controls gave measurably impoverished descriptions — 11.5 information units against 14.9 — so no threshold rule could have separated them, and the likely cause is task administration rather than the classifier (section 5.19). The pre-specified operating point remains 0.367, and the primary external result remains AUC 0.8533 [0.7371, 0.9458] at that threshold, carrying the exposure history of section 3.9 (section 5.3). What this chapter contributes is a diagnosis of why the specificity was what it was, and a rule that follows from the diagnosis, specified for the next iteration — not a better number for this one. The chapter reads in three movements. Sections 5.1 to 5.14 report the validated instrument — development performance and its confounds, the external validation and calibration, the scorer check against a human, the fifteen negative results in one place, the measurement properties, and the cross-corpus evidence behind the pooling decision. Sections 5.15 to 5.21 are the diagnosis of the specificity problem. Sections 5.22 to 5.30 are the probes that belong to the specified successor, reported under the rule of section 1.7 — never in a table with a validated figure. Four results matter most, and they are named here so that forty-one pages have a way in. First, no degradation was observed in the external point estimate, on a corpus collected by different investigators, at a threshold fixed in advance and a single scoring — a property most published systems in this field never test, though the evaluation was not designed as a formal non-inferiority study (section 5.3). Second, the 33.3% specificity is diagnosed rather than excused: the threshold was referenced to the wrong distribution, the false positives were genuinely thin descriptions, and the rule that follows — a threshold set on local healthy controls — delivers specificity to target in every cohort tested (sections 5.15 to 5.19). Third, the deployed picture-description architecture performs weakly for mild impairment — Delaware cannot be learned from its own recordings (section 5.12.1) — and within the available multi-task corpus, connected discourse is the more promising elicitation genre (section 5.25), the pair of findings that reshaped the successor battery. Fourth, the reproducibility of the screening decision itself — 0.8355, one decision in six reversing on a repeat recording — appears, within the literature identified by this search, not to be reported, and naming, measuring and bounding it is a contribution (section 5.26.1). The limitation that leads all others is the sex disparity of section 5.2.1: a specificity failure with a measured mechanism, and one that no threshold or rescaling can repair.

Modest and real is the accurate description.

### 5.1.2 The Search For A Better Number And What It Found

Whether anything within the project's constraints could improve the deployed figure was searched under four fixed conditions: deployed model, features and threshold unchanged; the external corpus left unscored; deployability preserved; and any gain real rather than an evaluation artefact. One real gain was found, and it is a protocol change rather than a model change. Averaging a participant's repeat recordings raises participant-level discrimination from 0.8095 at recording level to 0.8551 when every participant's scores are averaged — and section 5.9.1 supplies the mechanism, a standard error of measurement of 0.103 that averaging attacks directly. The qualification travels with it: single-visit participants alone already reach 0.8198, so part of the span is the removal of weighting toward frequent attenders, and section 5.24 decomposes how much of the remainder is measurement precision rather than disease progression. It is written as a deployment recommendation with a measured effect, not as a model improvement.

A pretrained-representation arm was run, and it neither refutes nor confirms a ceiling claim. Replacing all 64 handcrafted features with a 300-dimensional mean document vector from pretrained static word embeddings — the same cohort, architecture and folds — gives 0.8018 against the handcrafted 0.8095 on Pittsburgh, a difference of −0.0077, graded CEILING-CLAIM-QUALIFIED under its pre-registered criteria. Read in the project's favour but no further: sixty-four interpretable features match three hundred opaque dimensions, which defends the design — but a static, order-discarding representation is a floor, not a ceiling, and a contextual transformer would very likely do better. That gap is unmeasured and unmeasurable on this hardware — no accelerator, no compatible runtime, and weights that cannot arrive through the deployment channel — and it is reported as a hardware constraint, not as a judgement that transformers were not worth testing. The conclusion of the negative-results register is worded accordingly: a generic pretrained representation performs comparably, not better; contextual models remain the untested direction.

Hyperparameter tuning was considered and rejected, and the reason is the thesis's own argument. Any configuration selected on development performance inflates the development estimate, and the external corpus is spent, so the choice could never be validated. The defensible version — nested tuning inside training folds — was unaffordable at roughly 180 fits of a calibrated three-member ensemble in the time remaining, and the affordable version would have undermined the governance this project is built on. Both halves are stated: tuning was not skipped because it would not help, but because the only version worth reporting could not be afforded.

The conclusion of that search: under the four constraints the space is close to empty — a protocol change worth about 0.045, a pretrained representation that matches rather than beats, two inert features that cost nothing (section 3.2.2), a transcription artefact whose correction lowers the headline by 0.010, and a task-genre finding the deployed instrument cannot act on because its training corpus has healthy controls on one task only (section 5.25). The remaining gap to the best comparably-evaluated published result is about four accuracy points, and closing it means more features or contextual embeddings — both of which forfeit the paper fallback, the explainable indicators and the operator-facing reference ranges that are the point of the design. The lower number is taken, and the reasons are now measured, which is the difference between a concession and a result.

## 5.2 Confound Control

Age is the confound that would most easily counterfeit this instrument, and it was measured. On raw Pittsburgh, age alone classifies at 0.707, because the corpus's dementia group is about 6.6 years older than its controls by construction; after participant-level age and sex matching, age alone falls to 0.515 — chance — while the speech model holds at 0.798. The discrimination is in the speech, not the birth year. The failed remedy is as instructive as the successful control: residualising age out of the features looked like a fairness improvement until the residualised features were shown to still contain age at R² = 0.994 under grouped cross-validation, and the technique was discarded (negative result 3, section 5.8). Education could not be analysed at all — the Pittsburgh corpus stores the cognitive score in the field the transcription standard reserves for education (section 3.2), so the column is unusable.

### 5.2.1 The Sex Disparity And Its Mechanism

Subgroup performance by sex is reported here as standard, because it is routine for clinical prediction models and this project's own audit found it was the largest disparity the instrument carries. Table (5.3) gives the headline: on Pittsburgh out-of-fold predictions with participant-clustered bootstrap, female participants are classified at 0.8687 and male at 0.7041, a difference of +0.1646 [0.0535, 0.2736]; the female figure exceeded the male in 1,998 of 2,000 replicates. Delaware points the same way, 0.6425 against 0.5958. Sixteen AUC points is not a rounding difference: a screening instrument with that gap will miss more male cases at any shared threshold, and in deployment nobody would notice. Source: `results/reconstruction/phase0_free_f_capture.txt`, the committed reconstruction of this analysis.

| Group | Recordings | Prevalence | AUC | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Female | 343 | 0.55 | 0.8687 | [0.8217, 0.9108] |
| Male | 205 | 0.57 | 0.7041 | [0.6027, 0.8064] |

Part of the gap is severity composition, and that part is measurable. Within the impaired group, women in this cohort are more severely affected — MMSE 18.77 against 21.35, a difference of 2.58 points, Mann–Whitney p = 0.0002 — and 2.9 years older (72.6 against 69.7), while controls are equivalent (29.26 against 28.94). Since sensitivity falls steeply with MMSE (section 5.7), a female impaired group weighted toward severe cases is mechanically easier to detect. Composition explains less of the gap than first appears, however: across the committed matching procedure's ten seeds, age and sex matching shrinks the gap only to a mean of +0.140 (female 0.850, male 0.710; seed range +0.092 to +0.191), about a seventh of it, and the residual is the interesting part.

The residual is not a sensitivity failure. Sensitivity within MMSE band is equal or better for men in three of four bands, as Table (5.4) shows; what differs is specificity — 0.545 for female controls against 0.438 for male — and the two distributions compress toward each other for men from both directions: male controls score higher (mean 0.447 against 0.382) and impaired men score lower (0.586 against 0.641), which is exactly what lowers a ranking measure.

**Table (5.4) : Sensitivity at the deployed threshold by MMSE band and sex, Pittsburgh impaired recordings. Sensitivity within MMSE band is equal or better for men in three of four bands; the sex gap is a specificity failure, not a sensitivity failure.**

| MMSE band | Female | Male |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ 15 | 0.978 (n = 45) | 1.000 (n = 17) |
| 16–20 | 0.930 (n = 71) | 0.963 (n = 27) |

The mechanism is plain and has nothing to do with cognition. Healthy men in this cohort simply say less about the picture — 13.01 information units against 14.14 (p = 0.018), 3.43 actions against 3.95 (p = 0.0075), a scored proportion of 0.57 against 0.61 — while impaired men say more than impaired women (word count 103.9 against 90.6; 10.66 information units against 9.81). The model's dominant feature is information content (section 5.1.1), so a healthy man who is simply less expansive looks impaired, and a fluent impaired man looks healthy. Sex differences in narrative verbosity are well documented and benign; the instrument does not know the difference.

A threshold rule cannot fix this, and the test that shows it is counter-intuitive. Sex-stratified control-referenced thresholds at an 80% specificity target do equalise specificity — 0.799 female against 0.798 male — but the male threshold rises to 0.625 against the female 0.481, fewer impaired men clear it, and the sensitivity gap widens from 0.176 at the pooled control-referenced threshold to 0.348 under stratification. A discrimination gap cannot be thresholded away, exactly as section 5.15 establishes for Delaware; men are a discrimination problem, not a threshold problem. Within-sex rescaling does nothing either, as the invariance result of section 3.11 requires: z-scoring the dominant feature within sex moves the pooled figure by −0.0015 and the model score by −0.0052 — noise. The deployed pooled choice over-refers men (specificity 0.438 against 0.545) rather than under-detecting them. The observed error direction favours sensitivity over missed cases, but excess false positives remain an equity, anxiety and resource-use concern. That property was measured after the fact, not designed.

What follows for the thesis is stated once. This disparity leads the limitations; sex-stratified performance is reported wherever it can be computed; matching controls the composition of a comparison and does nothing about within-sex performance, so it is not an answer; and the fix is named as future work — a feature set that does not conflate said less with impaired, or sex-specific normative reference ranges, which the pilot's healthy arm can supply at no additional cost if it recruits both sexes in sufficient number. Two smaller observations from the same sweep close the section: performance is flat across transcript-length quartiles (0.781 / 0.822 / 0.820 / 0.797 on Pittsburgh), so the instrument is not merely measuring how much someone said even though information count is its strongest feature; and the education column remains unusable for the reason given in section 5.2.

## 5.3 External Validation

The claim is stability, not superiority, and it is led with rather than retreated to. The external evaluation does not demonstrate improved performance: with 53 participants, the interval on 0.8533 [0.7371, 0.9458] cannot be separated from the combined development figure of 0.7550 [0.7192, 0.7902], and an examiner who notices that a model apparently improved out of sample will rightly ask what is wrong with the development estimate. What the evaluation establishes is narrower: no degradation was observed in the external point estimate, on a corpus collected by different investigators, under a threshold fixed in advance and a single scoring. The evaluation was not designed as a formal non-inferiority study, and none is claimed. Most published models in this literature lose substantial performance out of sample; many never test it. ### 5.4.1 Calibration Meets The Bayesian Chain

A defect was found, the standard correction was derived, and a pre-registered test said do not apply it — a sequence worth more than either a defect or a fix alone. The defect: section 3.12 derives the likelihood ratio by dividing the model's odds by the training prevalence, a step exact only for a calibrated score, and section 5.4 has just said the score is not calibrated. With slope 1.2887 the derived ratio is pulled toward one and the speech evidence is systematically under-weighted. Table (5.7) bounds the size: up to 0.16 of posterior probability, always understating at the top.

**Table (5.7) : The under-weighting, bounded — likelihood ratios as deployed and corrected, with the posterior at age 80 to 84. The uncorrected likelihood ratio understates strong evidence by up to 0.16 of posterior probability and never overstates it.**

| Score | LR as deployed | LR corrected | Posterior, deployed → corrected |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0.125 | 0.076 | 0.083 → 0.052 |
| 0.367 | 0.651 | 0.638 | 0.320 → 0.316 |
| 0.70 | 2.62 | 3.84 | 0.655 → 0.735 |
| 0.85 | 6.36 | 12.04 | 0.822 → 0.897 |
| 0.95 | 21.3 | 57.3 | 0.939 → 0.977 |

The correction is a derivation, not a tuning exercise — two-coefficient logistic recalibration, maximum likelihood, no search — and because the fitted slope is positive it is strictly monotone, so discrimination, both operating points, the locked external result and the deployed decision would all have been invariant [1, 2, 3]. The obvious test was nonetheless forbidden: trying the correction on the locked corpus would be exactly the contamination section 3.9 documents, committed deliberately after writing that section. The decision was therefore made on development data alone, by cross-cohort transfer of the recalibration itself — fit the two coefficients on one development cohort, apply to the other, in both directions, under criteria fixed in writing beforehand: apply the correction only if it moves the slope toward one in both directions without worsening the Brier score by more than 0.005. Table (5.8) reports the result: it fails in both directions on both criteria, grade CORRECTION-DOES-NOT-TRANSFER.

**Table (5.8) : The pre-registered transfer test of the recalibration. The recalibration fails in both transfer directions on both criteria, grade CORRECTION-DOES-NOT-TRANSFER, because the two cohorts are miscalibrated in opposite directions.**

| Arm | Target slope, raw | After correction | Brier, raw → corrected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pittsburgh → Delaware | 0.823 | 0.543 | 0.2198 → 0.2251 |
| Delaware → Pittsburgh | 1.516 | 1.841 | 0.1815 → 0.1888 |

The reason is the finding. Pittsburgh's own slope is 1.516 and Delaware's is 0.823 — the two development cohorts are miscalibrated in opposite directions, the pooled 1.2887 is an average of two numbers pointing opposite ways, and no single recalibration can fit both, because the miscalibration is a property of the cohort and not of the model. This is the third independent line of evidence — after the threshold analysis of section 5.13 and the recalibration table of section 5.14 — that a single global map does not serve multiple cohorts, and it is a further argument for control-referenced thresholding, which is calibration-independent by construction.

The part written most carefully is the coda. The locked corpus was scored, descriptively, after the decision was made — under a declaration, fixed before execution, that whatever it showed would not enter the decision. On the external corpus the raw slope is 1.886, the most compressed of the three, and the development-fitted correction moves it to 1.464 — toward one, on both criteria the development arms failed. Had the locked corpus been consulted, the correction would have been applied; the rule that forbade consulting it changed the decision. That is a demonstration, on this project's own work, of what the rule is for. What is done, then: the arithmetic is unchanged; the defect, its magnitude, its direction and its containment are reported — it is a reporting error and not a triage error, since everyone above 0.367 is referred either way, and the banded output absorbs it in 13 of 14 representative comparisons, the exception being a score of 0.70 at age 65, which moves one band.

### 5.5.1 The Information Unit Scorer Checked Against A Human


The scorer behind the instrument's most important feature family had never been compared against a human, and the comparison was pre-registered before any score was entered or seen: twenty Pittsburgh transcripts, stratified across the range of automated counts, scored blind by hand against the 23-unit scheme, report-and-stop. The registered criterion was a mean absolute difference of at most 1.200 units per transcript. The result is 1.600, and the grade is INADEQUATE. The grade stands; what follows says what kind of failure it is.

It is a systematic undercount, not disagreement. The bias is −1.500 — the software credits fewer units than the human — with limits of agreement [−5.18, +2.18]; misses outnumber false credits 41 to 11, so the synonym lists are systematically too tight, and every one of the worst-agreeing units except the falling stool fails in that same direction, the water unit worst of all (nine misses, kappa 0.10). Yet the software agrees with the human about who scores higher than whom: intraclass correlation 0.904 — against a published human-to-human figure on this task of 0.347 [−0.30, 0.69] — Spearman 0.920, and 6.8% discordant pairs [4]. The offset is also flat, and a mechanism proposed in advance was refuted: if speakers who point rather than name were undercounted more, the bias would grow with impairment and distort the ranking, but the difference is uncorrelated with the human total (−0.005, p = 0.982), equal in both halves of the range (−1.60 against −1.40, p = 0.819), and uncorrelated with the pronoun-to-noun ratio (+0.063, p = 0.791). The prediction was wrong.

Four consequences follow. The rank-based results survive, by measurement rather than assumption — every AUC resting on these features depends on ordering, and the ordering is preserved. The absolute scale does not survive: the operator-facing reference ranges are calibrated against a scorer that runs about 1.5 units low. The paper fallback is directly affected, which is the deployment consequence — a health worker counting by hand will land about 1.5 units above the software for the same speech, so a threshold derived from software counts does not transfer to hand counts, and the paper instrument needs its own cut-off from hand-scored local controls, which is a second, independent argument for control-referenced thresholding. And the successor protocol needs what this check lacked: scorer training and double-scoring of a subsample, because one rater gives agreement, not human reliability — on the published figure, two humans disagree more on this task than this human disagreed with the software.

## 5.6 Analysis Across Tasks


The screening task was never chosen on evidence — the Pittsburgh corpus has healthy controls on picture description only — and the first attempt to put evidence behind the deployed four-task battery is reported here with its own correction, because the sequence is instructive. On Delaware, the single-task comparison of story retelling against picture description is observed, not proven: +0.069 [−0.017, +0.157], p = 0.122. The pre-registered battery test that followed found a five-task battery at 0.6455 [0.5763, 0.7154] against picture description alone at 0.5687 [0.4998, 0.6393] on the same 288 participants, paired difference +0.0769 [0.0015, 0.1505] — graded BATTERY-BETTER, an interval excluding zero narrowly. That grade does not survive two design defects, both this project's own, found after the first output was seen and disclosed as such. The participant labels were taken from the first row in file order, which is not the first visit, and for the 18 of 288 participants whose label changes across visits the arm used an arbitrary visit's label — the reported class split matches neither the ever-impaired nor the earliest-visit convention. And the battery arm carried 320 features against the single task's 64, so task count was confounded with column count. Section 5.25 redoes the comparison with both defects removed, and the battery gain does not survive: five tasks combined by score averaging do not beat the best single task (+0.0102 [−0.0579, +0.0818], BATTERY-NOT-CONFIRMED). The two results are not a contradiction, and they are not presented as a reversal: feature concatenation lets a model learn to down-weight a task that carries no signal, equal-weight averaging cannot, so read together they localise where the apparent battery gain lives — not in administering more tasks, but in being able to weight them — and the cheapest way to down-weight the weak task is not to administer it, because the weak task is the picture. The sentence this once licensed, that the four-task design "can now be defended as a measurement rather than a preference", is withdrawn, and it is reproduced here rather than deleted so a reader of an earlier draft can see exactly what changed.

Negative result 8 needs two sentences the others do not. ### 5.9.1 Visit To Visit Stability And The Minimal Detectable Change


No clinical instrument should be proposed without its measurement error, and this one did not have it until it was computed from the corpus's own structure: controls recorded at successive annual visits should not change, so their between-visit score movement estimates the instrument's noise. Among control pairs with gaps up to 1.5 years (123 pairs), the standard error of measurement is 0.103 and the minimal detectable change at 95% confidence is 0.286 (all gaps: 0.110 and 0.305). Read against the scale it lives on: the deployed threshold is 0.367 on a zero-to-one score, so a person's score can cross the threshold and move two reported bands with nothing about them having changed. This is a screening test and not a monitoring test, and 0.286 is the number that establishes it. The counterpart is the interesting half: impaired participants' scores rise +0.045 per visit (113 pairs) while controls' do not move at all, so progression is visible in aggregate and not in an individual. Two reasons this is an upper bound on the deployed error travel with it — the scores are out-of-fold, so a person's two visits were scored by different fold models, and the median gap is a year, so genuine change is inside the estimate. Visit-to-visit agreement among controls is 0.465, labelled exactly that — not test–retest reliability — and it is the conservative quantity for both this section and negative result 8.

### 5.12.1 Delaware Cannot Be Learned From Itself

Trained on Delaware alone and validated within Delaware, the same architecture reaches 0.547 [0.485, 0.605] — an interval spanning chance — while a model trained on Pittsburgh reaches 0.646 on the same cohort. The foreign model beats the native one. Within this cohort, connected-speech features carry no reliably learnable signal separating mild cognitive impairment from healthy ageing. That is a substantive negative claim about the reach of the whole method, not a quirk of one dataset, and it qualifies the project's central premise; it is also the single most interesting result in the thesis. Four explanations were candidates: intrinsic difficulty of the mild target, whose speech sits close to healthy; training-set size, since Pittsburgh supplies 548 recordings against roughly 351 per within-Delaware fold; instability of the MCI label itself, since MCI is not a stable state — a pooled analysis reports 15% reversion to normal across clinic and community samples, so a fraction of the labels belong to people who are, or will become, cognitively normal, and a classifier cannot learn a boundary the labels do not respect; and task administration, the mechanism section 5.19 documents on the external corpus, running the other way [6].

The second explanation was tested and is ruled out. Pittsburgh was subsampled by participant to Delaware's exact recording count and the identical within-cohort protocol re-run over three seeds: 0.8113, 0.8182 and 0.7953, mean 0.8083, against 0.8137 for full Pittsburgh — cutting Pittsburgh to Delaware's size costs about half a point of AUC, so Delaware's 0.547 is not a sample-size effect. The pre-registered grade is SIZE-RULED-OUT, and three explanations remain live: intrinsic difficulty, label instability and administration. This design cannot separate them, and writing as though it could would be exactly the overreach this project has spent its effort avoiding; the finding must also not be softened into "MCI is harder" — it is stronger and stranger than that, and it is the reason section 5.25's task-genre result matters. What this section replaces is also stated: the original pre-lock pooling comparison asked whether adding Delaware improves generalisation to an unseen corpus, which now needs a third two-class corpus that no longer exists; this is a weaker proposition, and it is not presented as a reproduction of that one.

## 5.13 Threshold Transportability

The model is frozen and 0.367 stands throughout; this section reads the stored predictions as data and asks whether one number can serve three cohorts. It cannot, and the single most important line in Table (5.16) says why: Delaware's impaired mean (0.409) is indistinguishable from Pittsburgh's healthy mean (0.406), and below the external cohort's healthy mean (0.471). ## 5.15 Two Specificity Problems Not One

The external corpus has a threshold problem: 33.3% specificity against a 77.8% ceiling — the most any threshold could deliver at 75% sensitivity — so roughly 44 points of achievable specificity were left untaken because that cohort's scores sit higher than development's. Delaware has a discrimination problem: at AUC 0.629 its ceiling at the same sensitivity is 0.376, so no threshold rule of any kind can help it, and 0.659 at the deployed threshold is above the ceiling only because the threshold buys specificity by surrendering sensitivity. Pittsburgh's ceiling is 0.770. This division is stated before any remedy, because it fixes which cohort each remedy can apply to: sections 5.16 to 5.18 address placement, and nothing in them rescues Delaware.

## 5.16 Threshold Efficiency

Every transported threshold rule is scored by recovery fraction — specificity achieved divided by the cohort's ceiling at the sensitivity actually reached — because raw specificity is not comparable across cohorts with different AUCs. Across all eighty cells (five rules by four sensitivity targets by four transfer directions) the median recovery fraction is 0.981, and 58 of 80 sit at 0.93 or above; restricted to the Lu-free development directions the median is 0.989. Figure (5.10) plots the cells. The rules were not inefficient — they landed in the wrong place. A fixed absolute threshold set for 75% sensitivity on Pittsburgh lands at 88.5% sensitivity on the external cohort, the percentile rule at 80.8%, mean-matching at 84.6%: specificity was spent on sensitivity nobody asked for, which is why the remedy is a referencing rule and not a better classifier. The boundary of the claim is checkable: 22 cells fall below 0.93, the lowest at 0.000, and every one is an overshoot cell where the rule ran to the far-left edge of the curve and the fraction became a ratio of near-zero quantities — failures of placement, not of efficiency.

## 5.17 Marginal Threshold Rules

Four rules that read only the target cohort's unlabelled score distribution — percentile, mean-matching, z-normalisation, quantile mapping — are diagnostics, not candidates for deployment. Two results establish that. Mean-matching collapses exactly where prevalence differs most: transported from Pittsburgh to Delaware at a 90% sensitivity target it lands at 99.4% sensitivity and 1.1% specificity, recovery 0.188, the single worst cell in the grid — because the difference in marginal means between those cohorts is mostly prevalence, not scale, and a rule that treats a prevalence difference as a calibration difference drags the threshold into the floor. And a percentile rule is arithmetically identical to fixing the referral rate — flag the top fraction, whatever the population — which transported acceptably here only because the external cohort's prevalence happens to sit within two points of development's. In a Libyan community clinic at 5% to 15% prevalence, a percentile rule would refer over half of everyone.

The prospective quantity has an exact, distribution-free answer: with the threshold at the k-th order statistic of n controls, prospective specificity follows a Beta(k, n+1−k) law, which for 27 controls gives 0.786 [0.62, 0.91] — appreciably wider, and the number a clinician actually cares about. Table (5.19) turns that law into the pilot's sample-size justification: a ±10-point interval needs about 59 healthy speakers, ±7.5 points about 108. The assumption the pilot will not satisfy is stated with its direction: the law assumes the control sample is drawn from the population the threshold will serve, and a healthier-than-typical convenience sample sets the threshold too low, which over-refers. The observed error direction favours sensitivity over missed cases, but excess false positives remain an equity, anxiety and resource-use concern.

## 5.23 A Minimum Length Rule Does Not Fix Specificity


The hypothesis came from the flagged controls themselves: the 215 development controls above the fixed threshold differ from the 307 correctly passed almost entirely in brevity — 103.2 words against 127.4, information-unit total 11.89 against 16.11 (standardised difference −1.19) — while age and cognitive score are near-identical, and the eleven strongest discriminators between flagged and passed controls are all information-unit counts. Declaring short recordings non-reportable, as standard instruments have validity criteria, would change no model, feature or threshold; it was pre-registered — grid, criteria and equity co-criterion fixed before execution — and it is negative result 10: GATE-NOT-JUSTIFIED. Table (5.24) shows why. Gates that keep enough of the sample buy almost nothing; the gate that buys most discards 44% of controls and 49% of the impaired for +0.069 specificity and −0.058 sensitivity; the AUC never leaves 0.739 to 0.757, so transcript length is not an artefact channel either; and the equity co-criterion passed, so the rule would not have been discriminatory — it simply does not work. ## 5.24 What Repeat Averaging Actually Buys

Averaging a participant's repeat recordings raises participant-level discrimination from 0.7643 at the first visit to 0.8467 — which looks like a decisive argument for a repeat-measurement protocol until the gain is decomposed, and the decomposition is negative result 11. Controls cannot progress, so averaging controls alone isolates pure noise reduction: it is worth +0.0076. Averaging the impaired alone — where the participant may genuinely have declined between visits — is worth +0.0565, seven times more. The Spearman–Brown check agrees from the other side: visit-to-visit agreement of 0.465 at 2.73 visits per participant predicts 0.8122 from noise reduction alone, and the observed 0.8467 exceeds it by +0.0345, pointing at disease progression rather than measurement precision. Most of the averaging gain is the disease, not the instrument — which forbids describing the +0.08 span as a measurement-precision result anywhere in this thesis. The fairness question was asked because nobody in this literature asks it: men gain more from averaging (+0.0915 against +0.0764) but the sex gap closes only from 0.2346 to 0.2196 — 6% of it — so repeat-averaging is not the fairness fix, and the deployment recommendation and the equity problem remain separate problems. One protocol consequence closes the section: at the fixed threshold, averaging shifts the whole distribution upward (specificity 0.533 to 0.446, sensitivity 0.797 to 0.920), so a repeat-averaging protocol needs its threshold re-derived — and control-referenced thresholding does that automatically, since a percentile of averaged controls is still a percentile.

## 5.26 What Repeat Sampling Can And Cannot Buy

Three questions, three different quantities. Does averaging k recordings cut measurement error by root-k? Yes, exactly — with the measured error of 0.103 the minimal detectable change falls from 0.286 at one recording to 0.202 at two and 0.090 at ten — but root-k assumes independent errors, two recordings taken minutes apart share the room and the speaker's day, and with error correlation 0.2 to 0.6 the two-recording figure is 0.222 to 0.256; the corpus contains no within-session repeats, so 0.202 is an upper bound on the benefit. Would that make individual tracking viable? No, and not nearly: the impaired group drifts +0.0372 per year, so detecting one year of average progression needs the error of 59 averaged recordings, and the screening-not-monitoring conclusion of section 5.9.1 survives averaging intact. What repeat sampling does buy is decision stability, the third quantity and the one that matters at the point of referral, and it has its own subsection.

### 5.26.1 Decision Reproducibility Is A Contribution

The probability that a fresh recording falls on the same side of the threshold as the first is 0.8355 — one screening decision in six would reverse on a repeat recording — rising to 0.8759 for the mean of two; within one standard error of the threshold it is 0.669, and outside that band 0.954 (`results/reconstruction/repeat_sampling_stdout_capture.txt`, `phase0_free_g_capture.txt`). Within the speech-based screening literature identified by this search, that number appears not to be reported, and the strongest available counter-case is the clearest confirmation: a 2024 study of remote speech assessment in fifty cognitively unimpaired adults, retested at two to three weeks, reports feature-level intraclass correlations from −0.06 to 0.97 — generally poor to moderate for a single sample, with multiple averaged sessions, four to five for the more demanding tasks, needed before most features reach 0.75 — and leaves open whether feature-level associations translate into stable classification decisions [8]. That open question is the one this figure answers, at the level of the thing the instrument actually outputs. A field that reports accuracy above 90% routinely and reliability almost never is a field whose accuracy figures have an unmeasured denominator; reporting 0.8355 does not fix that, but naming the quantity, giving the method — any study with repeat recordings can compute it — and supplying one worked value does. It is also the reason the three-band report exists: reproducibility is 0.954 away from the threshold and 0.669 near it, so the protocol stops forcing a binary in the region that cannot support one. At the recommended half-width of half a standard error, the band declines to call 23.7% of cases, raises sensitivity among the rest by +0.052 for −0.012 of specificity, and lifts decision reproducibility to 0.911; a second recording is offered for indeterminate results only — repeating everyone spends 100% of the sessions for +0.040 of reproducibility, repeating the grey zone spends 42% for 56% of that benefit — no repeat protocol is offered for monitoring, which section 5.26 forecloses; and the band is defined against the local control-referenced threshold, so it travels with the deployment rule of section 5.18 and needs no new validation study.

## 5.27 The Referential Deficit Index Off The Picture

This probe ran before the Arabic specification was written, not after, because if the battery moves to discourse (section 5.25) the index has to move with it. It does not — and the correct reading is broader than the registered question. Pre-registered, same 288 participants, same measures and matching rules as the English probe, sanity gate passed on all five tasks: the pronoun-free index clears chance on none of the five Delaware tasks — 0.535, 0.489, 0.570, 0.498, 0.460 — the genre contrast is directionally worse on discourse and includes zero (−0.0523 [−0.1098, +0.0054]), and the registered grade is RDI-DOES-NOT-TRAVEL. The established English pronoun marker is equally silent on all five. The task change is not what breaks the index; the target change is: its only positive evidence is the dementia cohort (0.596, matched 0.625, section 5.10.1), and in English the construct carries weak signal for dementia and none detectable for mild impairment, on any task. What carries the mild-impairment signal on discourse instead, measured over the same 43 shared features, is volume and structure: word count is the single strongest feature at 0.631 against 0.554 on picture description, with sentence count, lexical richness, dependency distance, semantic dispersion and reformulation behind it, while every referential measure sits at chance in both genres. On connected discourse, mild impairment presents as reduced output, thinner lexis, simpler syntax and weaker semantic progression — not referential vagueness — a coherent clinical picture and the opposite of what the index was designed to catch. This connects to negative result 9 and sharpens both: a picture bounds what there is to say, so volume is capped and near-uninformative (0.555); a narrative or a procedure does not, so volume becomes a measure of what the speaker can still generate. One protocol requirement follows immediately and is not optional: if word count is the strongest single marker, any protocol that lets elicitation time vary is measuring examiner behaviour, so the successor fixes the prompt, the maximum duration and the re-prompt rule, and records elicitation time — Chapter 6 carries it as a specification requirement.

Whether the stage dissociation was already known was searched, and the answer is consistent with the field, never stated as a finding. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 54 studies pools speech-based detection of mild cognitive impairment at 0.78 [0.70, 0.86] and names the informative domains as lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, acoustic properties and semantic content — the families found moving on discourse above — and does not isolate referential measures at all [9]. Where referential measures have been tested head-on they have not separated even dementia: an Italian study computing ten cohesion features, the pronoun-to-noun ratio among them, on twenty dementia patients and twenty matched controls found no referential measure significant after correction — only lemma repetition and an overall cohesion score survived [10]. The strongest apparent counter-case: a German study reports pronoun-use differences a decade before diagnosis, in six later-diagnosed cases against six matched controls, in biographical interviews, at group level — and its authors state the findings do not support individual-level discrimination [11]. The same standard applied throughout — a group difference is not a usable decision — yields: a real finding at the level it was measured, and not evidence that a referential measure can classify a person. The verdict is modest: the reviews' silence is absence of evidence; this probe is evidence of absence — directly measured, on five tasks, with the English marker and its pro-drop replacement side by side — the first direct test of the stage dissociation among the literature identified by this search, resting on one corpus, one target, n = 288. What it costs the Arabic contribution is stated where that contribution is specified: the index remains a defensible dementia marker on picture description, it is not supported as a mild-impairment marker, and Chapter 6 splits the specification by target rather than pretending one instrument serves both.

## 5.28 The Minimal Probe And A Claim Withdrawn Before It Reached The Thesis

The first version of this analysis said that counting words ties the calibrated ensemble on mild-impairment discourse. That claim is withdrawn; it was a selection artefact, and the sequence is recorded. What was measured: on the same 288 participants, hand-countable quantities on the two discourse tasks — total words 0.6420 [0.5786, 0.7052], total utterances 0.6249, words z-scored per task 0.6473 — against the 43-feature ensemble on the same tasks at 0.6379. Read naively, the count wins by 0.0094. The reading was wrong because the two sides were estimated by different procedures: the ensemble's architecture was fixed before the data were seen, while word count was the argmax over 43 features on these same participants, and the maximum of 43 noisy estimates exceeds the truth. Estimated alike — the best single feature chosen inside each training fold and scored on the held-out fold — the fair single-feature comparator is 0.6018, a selection optimism of +0.0455, and the fold-by-fold picks show no single feature is stably best: word count wins three folds of five, semantic progression and dispersion one each. The corrected comparison is the ensemble at 0.6379 against a fairly estimated comparator at 0.6018 — the apparatus buys +0.0361, not nothing — and section 6.1 sets this beside the same measurement on the development target. What survives: word count remains the right hand-computable measure on discourse, its true value on this population lies between about 0.60 and 0.647, one corpus cannot narrow that further, and the successor's power calculation runs on the conservative end, which roughly doubles the required sample. Why the withdrawal is reported: the withdrawn version was written and read before the check was run, deleting it would leave a cleaner section and a false record, and the defect was not in the measurement but in a comparison whose two sides were not estimated alike — the fourth instance of the lesson section 3.10 records, and the one the pre-registration template did not anticipate.

## 5.29 The Age Adjusted Posterior And Discrimination


The risk layer had been derived, verified mechanically over 1,440 property cases, and sourced — but its output had never been scored for discrimination, and the invariance result of section 3.11 does not dispose of the question, because the posterior is monotone in the score only at fixed age. The pre-registered test is negative result 14: on the combined development set the age-adjusted posterior gains +0.0163 [−0.0127, +0.0446] over the raw score — ADJUSTMENT-NEUTRAL. The one row that must never be quoted alone is Pittsburgh's, and the registration said so before it was computed: its apparent gain of +0.0356 [+0.0035, +0.0694] excludes zero and is not a gain — Pittsburgh's dementia group is 6.6 years older than its controls by construction, age alone reaches 0.707 there against 0.515 matched, and under age matching the gain reverses to −0.0346. The adjustment adds an age prior: where age carries label information it helps, where it does not it injects noise, and on Pittsburgh it was measuring the corpus. The layer's value is interpretive, not discriminative — converting a screening score into an age-appropriate absolute risk is a calibration job, and calibration and discrimination are different properties; the layer does what it was built to do, and that has now been measured. One deployment consequence: in an age-homogeneous local population the adjustment cannot help ranking and may cost a little; it is retained for what it reports. Family history could not be tested at all — no corpus in this project records it — so the multiplier rests on published relative risks and cannot be validated on any data available here, stated rather than worked around.

## 5.30 Vector Matching For Information Units

The scorer check of section 5.5.1 located the undercount in lists that are too tight, and the only pretrained model that runs on this hardware is a static word-vector table; whether similarity could close the gap is negative result 15. Pre-registered, nine declared thresholds, scored against the twenty hand-scored transcripts: the word-list baseline has a mean absolute difference of 0.550 with 7 false credits, and every vector threshold inflates false credits by an order of magnitude — 74 at the best threshold, 144 at the loosest — grade VECTORS-HURT. The reason generalises beyond the model size: static vectors encode topical relatedness, not synonymy, and everything in a picture description is topically related to everything else — that is what makes the picture one coherent scene — so within-scene similarity is uniformly high and no cut-off separates a synonym from a co-occurring object. A larger static model fails the same way by the same mechanism; contextual judgment is a different mechanism, and it needs a runtime this deployment target does not have. One reproduction gap is disclosed: the probe's baseline reproduced the committed extractor's decisions on 94.4% of unit-transcript pairs, and the committed baseline has fewer false credits than the reproduction, so the inflation under vectors would be worse, not better — the grade is unaffected. Where the undercount actually lives, from the committed features: both blocks undercount, and the six action units contribute nearly half the misses from a quarter of the units — so the remedy is better lists and a dependency-aware action rule, not a similarity threshold, and neither is deployable without changing the frozen model.
